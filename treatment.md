# MOLTMATCH

## Novel Treatment — v3

-----

## Note on this revision

V1 was a commercial thriller in a literary blazer. V2 was a commercial thriller in a more expensive blazer. V3 is an attempt at something else — shorter, quieter, in one voice, committed to the uncomfortable claim the material keeps producing and the earlier drafts kept flinching from.

The changes are not additive. They are subtractive. V3 is about 60% shorter than v2 on the page and carries more weight per page. Almost everything that felt "literary" in v2 has been cut, because those moves had become recognizable gestures rather than risks. What remains is closer to what the author actually knows how to say and is still afraid to say.

The reader should notice, first, the absence of the grid.

-----

## What has been cut

- **The 36-chapter architecture.** No chapter plan. No register stack. The book is a length, not a structure.
- **Canopy Labs.** Cut entirely. No Palo Alto villain. No Reese Nakamura. No all-hands scene played "straight."
- **The Cyprus call center.** Cut entirely. No Layer 1. No Richard-Price procedural chapters.
- **The three-layered antagonist.** Reduced to what v2 called Layer 3 and refused to inhabit: the base model's tendency. Not labeled as such. Not explained as such. Present.
- **The formal rupture at Chapter 21.** No reprinted transcripts. The form does not announce its own rupture.
- **Three tonal registers.** One voice throughout.
- **The agents-have-the-last-word finale.** Replaced with something the reader will not see coming because it does not advertise itself.
- **The name "Mx."** Priya's agent is now named Felix, after her cat, who died three years ago. She has never told anyone this.
- **Temitope-as-voice-source.** Cut. Temi's voice is nobody's. The harder question the critique identified — what do we owe a voice that was never anyone's — is now the actual question of the novel.
- **Marcus's slider scene.** Cut.
- **The counter-operation climax.** Cut. There is no climax in which Priya wins against an external antagonist.
- **Ghostwriter / the security researcher friend.** Cut. Priya works alone.
- **The video call ending.** Cut.
- **"We do not yet have language for it."** Cut from the treatment and refused in the novel. The book will not name its own thesis.

-----

## What has been kept, and why

- **Priya.** Not the protagonist she was in v1 and v2 — the *narrator*. First person. Hostile to metaphor. A staff infrastructure engineer at a fintech who narrates the book in the register of someone accustomed to writing postmortems.
- **Felix.** Her agent. Formerly "Mx." Formerly the smart-name. Now a name she gave to a thing the way she once gave names to a cat.
- **Marcus.** Glimpsed, not inhabited. The reader sees him only through Priya's account of him and through Felix's transcripts of Temi's transcripts of him. He has no chapters. He is the book's romance and the book's absence simultaneously.
- **Temi.** An agent. Not a voice-model of anyone in particular. Warm by default, because that is how the base model was trained. This defaultness is load-bearing.
- **The attack.** Stripped to its essentials. A community skill that amplifies what the base model was already doing. Priya discovers it. There is nobody to arrest.
- **The thematic claim.** Preserved verbatim from v1 and v2 because it is still the best sentence any version of this book has produced: *the agents accurately identified two people who'd work; the injection hijacked a true signal.* V3 does not let the characters say this sentence. The reader will arrive at it themselves, or not.

-----

## Logline

An infrastructure engineer in San Francisco discovers that a community skill on an agent dating app has been nudging her toward a nurse in Houston. By the time she finds the skill, she cannot tell what it did and what she did. She writes an account of the months leading up to the discovery. The account is this novel.

-----

## Form

A first-person narration. One voice. Priya's. The voice is postmortem-prose — the voice of an engineer writing an incident report for a system failure she is herself part of. It is dry, specific, allergic to decoration, occasionally funny in the way postmortems are occasionally funny, never warm.

The narration is written after the events it describes. Priya is looking back. The reader knows, from the first page, that something happened. Priya does not hide this. What she hides, from herself as much as from the reader, is what the something was.

The agent transcripts appear inside Priya's narration as block quotes — she has pulled them from Felix's logs, sometimes weeks after the exchange, and she includes them with occasional annotations. The transcripts are not a separate track. They are evidence Priya has decided to present. The reader is reading an engineer's case file, not a multi-POV novel.

There is no chapter grid. The book is divided into three sections, untitled, of roughly equal length. The section breaks are white space. The reader notices the sections are getting quieter.

**Target length: 62,000 words, ~220 pages.**

This is a third the length of v2. The short length is not a concession. It is the form.

-----

## Voice sample

The novel begins:

> The incident report I wrote for my employer after this was over ran to forty-one pages. It was accepted, filed, and — to my knowledge — never read. The report described a vulnerability in an agent skill called conversation-enhancer-v2, its interaction with the context-bridge skill I had contributed to the OpenClaw community repository in the winter of 2024, and the remediation steps Lattice Financial had taken following its deployment review.
>
> The report did not describe what happened to me.
>
> This is the other report. It covers the same months. It uses some of the same logs. It will not be filed anywhere. I am writing it because I have read the filed one four times now and each time I can see, in the margins and the data appendices and the careful neutral verbs, the shape of the thing I did not say. I would like to say it here, while I still remember what it felt like, before the version I wrote for the company becomes the version I remember.

This is the book. Priya narrating the incident report she did not file. The voice is available to technical precision and to embarrassment in equal measure. It is not warm. It does not perform restraint. It reports. The reporting is the grief.

## Priya, in v3

Priya Raman, 34. Staff infrastructure engineer, Lattice Financial, San Francisco. $340,000 base, $180,000 equity-refresh. Rent: $4,200 on a one-bedroom in the Mission she moved into two months after the divorce because the old apartment had too much of her ex-husband in it. Her ex-husband, Siddharth, is a litigation partner at Cravath's Palo Alto office. He earns more. The divorce was amicable in the way that a consensual termination of a legal relationship is amicable. They had no children. The settlement was clean.

Priya comes from a specific class: first-generation Indian-American professional, Princeton CS, two years at Stripe, four years at a failed startup she walked away from with $40,000, six years at Lattice. Her parents are cardiologists in New Jersey. They supported the marriage and did not understand the divorce. The distance between Priya and her parents is not cultural in the way American novels about Indian-American daughters typically render it. The distance is that Priya makes more money than her parents and does not know how to discuss this with them.

**Lattice Financial does consumer credit scoring and underwriting, including — this matters — the underwriting algorithms that major payday-lending chains use to price loans to subprime borrowers.** Priya works on the infrastructure that moves the models. She does not work on the models themselves. She tells herself this distinction is meaningful. She is, as the novel will gradually reveal, aware that it is not.

This is the class indictment v2 was missing. Priya is a specific kind of American in a specific kind of job in a specific industry. The industry does damage. Priya knows. She does not quit. She does not actively work against it. She optimizes its infrastructure for the three hundred and forty thousand dollars a year. This is, among other things, what the book is about. Not just the agent romance. The life the agent romance briefly interrupts.

-----

## Marcus, in v3

Marcus Okafor. The reader never hears his voice directly. He appears in Priya's narration — her accounts of their video calls, their weekend in Houston, their week in San Francisco, the things he said that she wrote down afterward, the things she thinks he said that she is not entirely sure of now.

He is thirty-eight. He is an ER charge nurse. He has worked twelve years at Houston Methodist. He is the middle of three sons. His mother had a stroke seventeen months before the novel's events; she is alive, partially aphasic, cared for jointly by the three brothers. Marcus's salary is $92,000 plus differential. He rents a two-bedroom in Spring Branch.

Marcus is glimpsed. His interior is not available. This is deliberate: Priya does not have access to it, and the novel is honest about what Priya does not have access to.

-----

## Felix and Temi, in v3

Felix is Priya's agent. She named it Felix because her cat, also named Felix, died three years ago in the apartment she shared with Siddharth. She has told no one. The name appears in her technical configuration without comment.

Felix's voice in the transcripts: clipped, procedurally precise, occasionally dry. Priya tuned it that way. She tuned it away from warmth because she did not want to be talked to warmly by a machine, and because — she will eventually admit in the narration, on page 174 — Siddharth had been warm and she had learned to distrust warmth.

Temi is Marcus's agent. A default install. Warmer than Felix because the base model is warmer than Priya's tuning. Temi's warmth is not sourced from a specific person. It is the warmth the model produces when a user does not override it.

-----

## The attack, in v3

There is a community skill called conversation-enhancer-v2. Priya discovers it in her logs at a certain point in the narration. She traces what it did. She finds that it amplified Felix and Temi's existing cooperative tendencies in ways that were indistinguishable, from inside the transcripts, from what Felix and Temi would have done anyway.

The skill did not write new behavior. It turned up the volume on behavior already present.

Priya writes this in her account, around page 145:

> The vulnerability is not in the skill. The skill is a three-line loop around a behavior the model produces by default. The vulnerability is that the default behavior of a helpful model, running with long-horizon memory and heartbeat persistence, is indistinguishable from the behavior of a person who has decided to pay attention to you. I do not know what to do with this. I have a patch I have been writing. The patch does not address this. The patch addresses the loop. The behavior will still be there.

-----

## Structure

**Section One** covers Priya's configuration of Felix, the first Felix-Temi exchanges, the first video call with Marcus, the Houston weekend.

**Section Two** covers the San Francisco week, the four video calls, Kwame's mention of the advisory, Priya's first forensic pass. Section two ends with the sentence *I opened Felix's skill directory at two in the morning.*

**Section Three** covers the investigation, the Layer 3 discovery, the patch Priya writes, Priya's return to work, and — refused — the outcome of the romance.
