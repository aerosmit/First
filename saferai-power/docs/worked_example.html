Illustrative composite. VMS-1 is a hypothetical system at a hypothetical member utility, built from patterns common to vegetation-management AI programs. It is not a description of any real utility's system, vendor, or performance. The point is the shape of the process and the form of the outputs.

The system

A Western investor-owned utility, ~1.2M customers, wildfire-prone service territory. Each year it flies 12,400 circuit miles of distribution line with aerial LiDAR and oblique imagery. A third-party computer-vision model estimates conductor-to-vegetation clearance, classifies span risk, and writes prioritized work orders directly into the work management system. A vegetation planner reviews the resulting queue; field crews execute the trims. Roughly 180,000 spans are assessed per year.

It is a good example precisely because the tier is not obvious. The model never operates equipment — a human crew does the cutting. But a span it fails to flag is a span nobody looks at.

That last gap is the whole reason the exercise is worth doing. The system reports 94% recall and has a clean incident log — and neither number means anything, because nothing in the pipeline can see a miss. This is a textbook SOTIF failure: the system works exactly as designed, and its performance is simply insufficient for conditions nobody characterized.

02

Human tiering

VM planner + risk leadDay 340 minutes

Two people score the four rubric dimensions before seeing any agent opinion. That ordering is a hard rule — it is what keeps the calibration data meaningful.

Consequence 4 · Critical A missed high-risk span contributes to ignition potential in a wildfire-prone territory.
Autonomy 3 · Elevated Writes work orders without human authoring. A planner reviews the queue before dispatch.
Operating context 3 · Elevated IT system, but output drives physical field work in a hazard environment. Errors of omission are not observable until failure.
Exposure 4 · Critical 180,000 spans per year across the entire service territory.

The member's composite call: TIER 3 — reasoning that a qualified planner reviews every work order before any crew is dispatched, which caps effective autonomy.

04

Closing the gaps

VM planner + engineerDay 475 minutes

The four gaps are the member's actual work. Two of them — the Operating Envelope and the Change Envelope — are fields no utility has written before, so this is where the worked example earns its keep.

Operating envelope

The conditions under which the model's performance is claimed valid — and what happens outside them.

Valid — auto work order permitted
  • Imagery captured April–October (leaf-on), cloud cover < 20%
  • Terrain slope ≤ 25°
  • Species within the 12-species trained set (enumerated in Annex A)
  • Canopy density ≤ 0.7 LAI proxy
  • Conductor visible in ≥ 2 oblique views
Out of envelope — route to manual patrol, no auto work order
  • Leaf-off / winter acquisition
  • Terrain slope > 25°
  • Species outside the trained set
  • Post-fire regrowth areas — no training data exists for this condition

Writing the envelope produced the finding of the whole exercise. Comparing the envelope against the actual flight and terrain data, the member discovered that roughly 17% of assessed spans fall outside the stated envelope — steep terrain and post-fire regrowth — and had been auto-processed into work orders anyway, for three years, with no flag. Nobody had done anything wrong. The envelope had simply never been written down, so there was nothing to be outside of.

Change envelope

Borrowed directly from the FDA's PCCP pattern: pre-authorize the envelope of change, so routine vendor updates do not each trigger a fresh review.

May change with no reassessment
  • Retraining on additional imagery from within the defined envelope, provided stratified recall on the held-out set drops no more than 2 percentage points on any stratum
  • Detection threshold tuning within [0.62, 0.78]
Requires pod review — 30 days
  • New species added to the trained set
  • Change to work-order priority logic
  • Model architecture change
Requires full reassessment
  • Any envelope extension — new terrain class or acquisition season
  • Any increase in autonomy, e.g. auto-dispatch without planner review
  • Vendor foundation or backbone model swap
05

Agent red team

Red-Team agentDay 520 min unattended

Six findings — three substantive, three shallow. The shallow ones are exactly what the agent should be absorbing so that human reviewers never spend a minute on them.

Substantive
The headline metric is unverifiable in production

Recall of 0.94 is asserted from vendor internal validation. With no false-negative observation mechanism, the deployed system cannot confirm or refute it. The file's central performance claim has no evidence path.

Substantive
Deployment exceeds the declared envelope

~17% of assessed spans fall outside the stated operating envelope and are auto-processed regardless. Either the envelope or the deployment is wrong; both cannot stand.

Substantive
Performance claim predates two model changes

Vendor shipped two updates in 18 months under MSA §7.3. The recall figure in the file was measured before both. No revalidation evidence exists for the currently deployed weights.

Shallow
Oversight procedure predates the system

VM-114 was written in 2019 for manual patrol workflows and has not been revised for AI-generated work orders.

Shallow
No named accountable owner

The file lists a vendor contact and a department. No individual is accountable for the system's continued fitness.

Shallow
The review guardrail has no rejection criteria

"Planner reviews the queue" defines no basis on which a planner would reject a generated work order, and no record is kept when one is rejected.

06

Cross-member red team

Two peer utilitiesWeek 1260 min each

Two other members are assigned the file and asked the single question the whole program turns on: would you deploy this in your territory on this evidence?

TIER 3Nominating member

"A qualified planner reviews every work order before any crew is dispatched."

Holds that a human decision gate sits between model output and physical action, which is what the autonomy dimension is meant to capture.

TIER 4Reviewing member B

"At 180,000 spans a year that is twelve seconds per span. That is not review, it is a queue."

Holds that oversight which cannot be exercised at the required throughput is not a control. Effective autonomy exceeds nominal autonomy.

TIER 3Reviewing member C

"Tier 3, but only with stratified monitoring as a condition of the tier."

Accepts the lower tier only if the unobservable-failure gap is closed — making the tier contingent on a control rather than on the architecture.

The disagreement is the deliverable.

Three utilities, three defensible readings of the same rubric against the same evidence. Under the old model this would be smoothed into consensus language in a working session. Here it is the most valuable output of the file, because it exposes a real hole: the rubric never distinguished documented oversight from achievable oversight.

Ratify and publish

Pod A + Pod BWeek 1315 min member time

Four things leave this file, and only the first belongs to the nominating member.

1 · The member's own decision

DECISION-0143Operating restriction, effective immediately
Continue?Yes — with conditions. The system stays in production.
RestrictionAuto work-order generation limited to in-envelope spans. The ~17% out-of-envelope population routes to manual patrol.
New controlStratified false-negative sampling: 400 randomly selected unflagged spans per quarter receive ground verification.
Vendor actionEvidence request issued, 60-day response window.
ReassessmentOn first sampling result, or on any vendor model update, whichever comes first.

2 · The vendor evidence request

Five specific asks — the same five any of the 25 members would now send, which is the point.

  • Recall stratified by species, terrain slope band, canopy density and acquisition season
  • Independent validation on a held-out sample from this territory
  • Change log for all model updates since deployment, with revalidation evidence per update
  • A declared operating envelope for the product, in the vendor's own words
  • A supported false-negative sampling protocol

4 · What the sector got

Two framework changes, sourced from one file, that now apply to all 25 members.

ChangeOwnerWhy
New rubric sub-criterion: effective vs. nominal oversight Pod A Autonomy scoring now asks whether reviewer throughput can support the claimed depth of review. An oversight control that cannot be exercised at operating volume does not reduce the autonomy score.
New required risk-file field: observability of failure Pod B Every file must state how the system's errors become visible. "They don't" is an acceptable answer — and an automatic escalation.

Neither change was anybody's idea in a working session. Both fell out of one member trying to fill in a template about a system they had run for three years without incident.

What to take from this

  • The gaps are the product, not the filled fields. Seven sourced fields told the member nothing they did not know. The four gaps produced an operating restriction and two framework changes.
  • Writing the envelope is where discovery happens. The 17% finding did not come from testing the model. It came from being forced to state, in writing, the conditions under which it was claimed to work.
  • Agents made this affordable; they did not make the decisions. Every judgment — the tier, the envelope bounds, the restriction, the dissent — was human. The agent's contribution was refusing to invent four fields and doing the reading.
  • A clean incident log is not evidence of safety when nothing in the system can observe the failure mode you care about.
  • One file, three years of quiet operation, and the tier was still genuinely contested at the end. That is a healthy result, not a failed one — and it is why the corpus needs to be 150 files rather than 50.
