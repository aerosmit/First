01 What it is

An assurance program, not a research program

SAFERai.power builds the operational risk framework, evidence templates and open-source tooling utilities need before AI is put into utility workflows. Its sibling, OPAI, is the build side. This is the assure side.

02 The distinction

Draw this line in the first two minutes of every briefing

There is a large, fast-moving 2026 regulatory workstream about AI and the grid that is not this. Executives who conflate them disengage — they hear “AI and the grid,” map it to the data-center interconnection fight, and assume it is covered.

03 The constraint

The whole expert budget is about one person-year

= 2,000 SME hours

Each participating organization contributes roughly 80 hours of skilled SME time, and participation qualifies for Self Direct Funds — which removes the “we have no budget line for this” objection almost entirely and should lead every membership conversation.

But the hours are the real constraint, and they rule out the default mode of industry consortia: convening working groups to author documents from a blank page.

2,000 total SME hours available across the entire founding cohort
One person-year does not write a standard. One person-year can harvest, reconcile, test and ratify material that already exists.

Print that number at the top of every planning document. Everything else in this deck is a consequence of it.

Every hour spent drafting prose that already exists elsewhere is an hour stolen from the one thing the group is uniquely able to do.

05 The MVP

Tier & File v1.0 — and nothing more

MVP = a use-case tiering rubric and a minimum evidence package (the AI System Risk File), calibrated against ~50 real utility AI use cases contributed by member companies.

Not the registry. Not the open-source toolkit. Not the monitoring runbooks. Not a certification scheme. The MVP must be the thinnest artifact that changes a real decision — and Tier & File does, on day one:

  • A utility can classify a use case and know what evidence to demand — the question every member is being asked internally right now, with no good answer.
  • Twenty-five utilities asking a vendor for the same evidence package is a market-moving act that costs the group nothing beyond agreeing on the template.
  • It needs no software, no infrastructure and no funding beyond SME time — so procurement cannot block it.

Agentic AI is the only lever that moves the constraint

More members adds coordination cost about as fast as it adds hours. More meetings consumes the budget rather than extending it. Only automating the non-judgment work actually changes the number — and the division of labour here is unusually clean.

Say this out loud in v1.0

IEC 61508 / 61511: AI is out of scope for safety instrumented functions

AI has historically been disallowed as a technique for achieving functional safety, and SIL certification does not accommodate ML. Assume any AI touching a safety instrumented function is out of scope for now — and say so explicitly.

A clear exclusion boundary is itself a valuable deliverable.

The most reusable structural idea in the whole set

IEC 62443 zones and conduits

An AI agent is a thing that lives in a zone and communicates through a conduit with defined, enforceable constraints. Lean on it hard for the guardrail catalog.

12 Containment

Where an AI system may live, and what it may touch

IEC 62443's zones-and-conduits model already exists in every utility's OT security architecture. It is the natural mechanism for containing AI, and it needs no new vocabulary.

14 Steal 1 · automotive

UL 4600 — standardize the argument, not the system

It solved the exact problem SAFERai faces: how do you write a standard for a system whose behavior you cannot fully specify in advance?

Its answer is to standardize the argument. You must make a structured, evidence-backed safety case, and the standard governs the shape and rigor of that argument rather than the design — taking the outputs of ISO 26262, 21448, IEC 61508 and MIL-STD-882 as inputs rather than competing with them.

The AI System Risk File should be structured as a UL 4600-style safety case — a claim about fitness for a defined use, an argument, and the evidence supporting it — with the framework specifying required claims and acceptable evidence types by tier.

Companion concepts to import with it

  • ISO 21448 (SOTIF) — hazards arising when the system works exactly as designed but its performance is insufficient for the situation. The dominant AI failure mode in utility work, with no natural home in NIST AI RMF language.
  • Operational Design Domain — the explicitly bounded conditions under which the system is claimed to work. (Track ISO/PAS 8800 alongside it.)

Rename it and make it mandatory

ODD → “Operating Envelope”

Most utility AI incidents will be out-of-envelope operation, and no current utility practice names the envelope.

15 Steal 2 · medical devices

FDA PCCP — the only mature answer to models that change

The hardest unsolved problem in regulated AI is not initial approval. It is that models get retrained, vendors ship updates, and foundation models change underneath you. Every static approval regime breaks on contact with this.

Adopt the PCCP pattern as a first-class SAFERai artifact. Without a change envelope, the framework will be abandoned within 18 months under its own review burden. Pair it with the GMLP 10 guiding principles — short, readable, sector-neutral, immediately adaptable.

16 Steals 3 & 4 · aviation, nuclear, banking

What makes data flow, and what makes validation proportional

Steal 3 — ASIAS + INPO · highest cultural value

ASIAS (FAA, since 2007) fuses voluntarily reported safety data across government and industry on one governing principle: information is used only for safety purposes, never for punitive action. That protection is what makes the data flow. INPO, created after Three Mile Island, runs confidential peer review, shared performance indicators, and EPIX.

Get the data-sharing protections drafted before the first incident, not after. Utilities will not share AI incidents into a forum containing their regulator unless protection is explicit and legally structured. This is legal work, not technical work, and it has the longest lead time of anything in the program.

The nuclear industry's post-TMI arc is also the right narrative for executive sponsorship: the industry built INPO voluntarily because the alternative was worse.

Steal 4 — SR 11-7 → SR 26-2 · highest process value

  • Validation quality now depends on the rigor of the review rather than organizational structure — a pragmatic loosening that utilities, with far thinner validation benches than banks, should copy directly.
  • Data is elevated to first-class risk: validation must reach training data, lineage and representativeness, not just the fitted model.
  • Model definition tightened to complex systems, explicitly excluding spreadsheets, deterministic rules and ordinary software. A crisp scope boundary is exactly what utilities need.
SR 26-2 explicitly places generative and agentic AI out of scope — “novel and rapidly evolving.” US banking regulators looked at governing generative and agentic AI and declined. SAFERai.power can occupy exactly that vacated ground with sector specificity — and credibly claim to be ahead of banking, which is a useful thing to be able to tell a board.

The single most transferable artifact is the model inventory. SAFERai's AI System Registry is the same object. Copy the field structure.

21 The mechanism

If one practice is imported from this work, make it this one

Weeks 17–20 · each member is assigned another member's files

“Would you deploy this system in your territory on this evidence? If not, what is missing?”

  • It converts vague standard-writing into a concrete, answerable decision. People argue endlessly about principles and decide quickly about artifacts.
  • It surfaces the real acceptance thresholds, which are tacit and never survive being asked for in the abstract.
  • It builds the peer-review muscle INPO proved is the durable mechanism in a safety-critical industry.
  • It produces the calibration data that turns a rubric into a standard.
Where two utilities disagree about whether the evidence is sufficient, that is the finding. Those disagreements are the sector's actual open questions, and enumerating them is more valuable than any amount of smooth consensus prose.

Seen in Part III

VMS-1 produced a three-way split on tier between the nominating member and two reviewers. Under the old model that gets smoothed into consensus language in a working session. Here it exposed a real hole — the rubric never distinguished documented oversight from achievable oversight — and became a framework change for all 25 members.

26 Fleet risk

Three ways the fleet goes wrong — one of them is invisible

1 · Convergence collapse — the serious one

If all 25 members use the same model to draft their risk files, the outputs correlate. The headline acceptance criterion — two independent utilities tier the same use case identically ≥ 80% of the time — then measures model agreement, not sector consensus, and the number looks excellent while meaning nothing. It is subtle, easy to miss, and would quietly invalidate the group's central calibration metric.

2 · Citation laundering

Plausible-sounding references to clauses that do not exist — severe here because the group's product is standards mappings, and a fabricated clause could propagate into a utility's compliance argument.

3 · Agent-generated consensus

An agent asked to summarize member positions will smooth them into agreement, because that is what summarization does — destroying the most valuable output of the program.

Countermeasures — all cheap, all week-two decisions

  • Tag every risk file with provenance: manual, agent-assisted, agent-drafted.
  • Compute inter-rater reliability within and across provenance classes separately, and report all three numbers. Divergence is the diagnostic.
  • Require the Tiering Copilot to produce the second opinion only.
  • Deliberately run a mixed-model fleet across pods rather than standardizing on one.
  • Hold back a manual-only control set of ~10 use cases as ground truth.
  • Source locators mandatory, spot-audited by the red team; low-confidence mappings blocked from publication.
Model neutrality is non-negotiable. Microsoft and NVIDIA are founding members. If the fleet couples to one vendor's models, the framework's neutrality is compromised and the mixed-model countermeasure becomes unavailable.

29 Durability

A framework written against 2026 systems misleads against 2028 systems

The hard problem is not writing v1.0. It is that AI capability moves faster than any standards process. Four mechanisms, then the one that matters most.

1

A change envelope for the framework itself

Apply PCCP logic reflexively. Editorial updates need no ratification; thresholds and evidence fields get a 30-day pod review; tier definitions, scope boundary and autonomy ladder need full ratification. Plus predefined triggers that force reassessment regardless of schedule.

Without this, v1.0 sits untouched for two years and is then replaced wholesale. Nearly every industry framework fails this way.

2

Capability-triggered review

“We revisit annually” is the wrong trigger — capability does not arrive annually. Define thresholds that automatically force re-tiering:

  • A model class gains a materially new capability in OT contexts
  • A deployed system's autonomy level increases
  • A new modality enters the operating environment
  • A vendor's foundation model changes materially
  • A sector incident reveals a failure mode outside the taxonomy

Each trigger names an owner and a clock.

3

A standing red team

2–3 people, refreshed quarterly across members, whose standing job is to break the framework rather than the systems: find use cases the rubric tiers absurdly, evidence packages that pass while the system is obviously unsafe, guardrails that are theatre.

Findings published inside the consortium as first-class artifacts. A framework with a documented list of its own weaknesses is far more trustworthy than one claiming completeness.

4

Horizon scanning on existing rails

EPRI already runs a Technology Radar and Pulse Report. Do not build a parallel one — attach an AI-assurance lens, with a standing quarterly item into Steering covering model capability, regulatory movement, cross-sector standards progress and adjacent-sector incidents.

Adjacent-sector incidents are the cheapest possible learning. Aviation, medical and financial AI failures are public, well-investigated and structurally similar. Reading someone else's accident report costs an hour.

30 Durability · the deepest point

Guardrails scale better than pre-deployment standards

In a fast-moving field, runtime guardrails scale better than pre-deployment standards.

Pre-deployment assurance assumes you can characterize the system before it operates. That assumption weakens every year as systems become more general and more agentic. Runtime guardrails — bounded permissions, output validation, human confirmation gates, circuit breakers, rate limits, IEC 62443 zone containment — do not depend on predicting behavior. They constrain consequences regardless of behavior.

Guardrails cost very little speed. Bounded agents constrained by validation and optional human approval have been measured running 13–18× faster than manual operation — the empirical point to put in front of an operations executive who expects assurance to mean delay.

The Tier 4 question

Not “how well was this model validated?” but “what is the worst thing this system can do before a human stops it, and is that acceptable?”

Answerable today, stays answerable as capability grows, and is exactly the question a utility operations leader already knows how to reason about.

Part III

One use case, end to end

A vegetation-management AI system through intake, agent pre-fill, tiering, gap-closing, red team and ratification — the artifacts produced at each step, and the finding that changed the framework for everyone else.

32 The system

VMS-1 · vegetation encroachment detection

A Western investor-owned utility, ~1.2M customers, wildfire-prone service territory. Each year it flies 12,400 circuit miles of distribution line with aerial LiDAR and oblique imagery. A third-party computer-vision model estimates conductor-to-vegetation clearance, classifies span risk, and writes prioritized work orders directly into the work management system. A vegetation planner reviews the resulting queue; field crews execute the trims. Roughly 180,000 spans are assessed per year.

It is a good example precisely because the tier is not obvious. The model never operates equipment — a human crew does the cutting. But a span it fails to flag is a span nobody looks at.

35 Stage 01 · the refusals

Four fields the agent refused to fill

The gaps are the product.

Gap · Operating envelope

No reviewed document defines conditions under which model performance is claimed valid. Vendor reports aggregate metrics only — not stratified by species, terrain slope, canopy density or acquisition season.

Gap · TEVV performed

Vendor internal validation only. No independent test on this utility's territory. No stratified recall reported.

Gap · Change envelope

MSA §7.3 permits vendor model updates on 30 days' notice with no revalidation requirement. Two updates occurred in the last 18 months. No reassessment was performed.

Gap · Monitoring plan

WMS records work-order completion. No mechanism observes spans the model did not flag. False negatives are structurally unobservable.

That last gap is the whole reason the exercise is worth doing. The system reports 94% recall and has a clean incident log — and neither number means anything, because nothing in the pipeline can see a miss. This is a textbook SOTIF failure: the system works exactly as designed, and its performance is simply insufficient for conditions nobody characterized.

36 The mechanism behind the gap

The loop that never closes

What the file claims

Recall 0.94, precision 0.91, and an incident log with no AI-attributed entries in three years of operation.

What can actually be observed

Completions of work orders the model generated. Nothing about spans it stayed silent on — and the WMS has no field to attribute an incident to the model at all.

What it produced

The new required risk-file field, observability of failure, and a quarterly ground-verification sample of 400 unflagged spans.

39 Stage 04 · VM planner + engineer · 75 minutes

Writing the envelope produced the finding of the whole exercise

The Operating Envelope and the Change Envelope are fields no utility has written before. This is where the worked example earns its keep.

Valid — auto work order permitted

  • Imagery captured April–October (leaf-on), cloud cover < 20%
  • Terrain slope ≤ 25°
  • Species within the 12-species trained set
  • Canopy density ≤ 0.7 LAI proxy
  • Conductor visible in ≥ 2 oblique views

Out of envelope — manual patrol, no auto work order

  • Leaf-off / winter acquisition
  • Terrain slope > 25°
  • Species outside the trained set
  • Post-fire regrowth areas — no training data exists for this condition
Nobody had done anything wrong. The envelope had simply never been written down, so there was nothing to be outside of.

40 Stage 04 · the change envelope

Borrowed directly from the FDA's PCCP pattern

Pre-authorize the envelope of change, so routine vendor updates do not each trigger a fresh review.

May change with no reassessment

  • Retraining on additional imagery from within the defined envelope, provided stratified recall on the held-out set drops no more than 2 percentage points on any stratum
  • Detection threshold tuning within [0.62, 0.78]

Requires pod review — 30 days

  • New species added to the trained set
  • Change to work-order priority logic
  • Model architecture change

Requires full reassessment

  • Any envelope extension — new terrain class or acquisition season
  • Any increase in autonomy, e.g. auto-dispatch without planner review
  • Vendor foundation or backbone model swap
Before this field existed, MSA §7.3 governed model change — vendor updates on 30 days' notice with no revalidation requirement, and two updates already shipped unreviewed. The change envelope is what converts a contractual notice period into an assurance control.

41 Stage 05 · Red-Team agent · 20 min unattended

Three substantive findings, three shallow ones absorbed

The shallow findings are exactly what the agent should be clearing, so that human reviewers never spend a minute on them.

Substantive — routed to the member

The headline metric is unverifiable in production

Recall of 0.94 is asserted from vendor internal validation. With no false-negative observation mechanism, the deployed system cannot confirm or refute it. The file's central performance claim has no evidence path.

Deployment exceeds the declared envelope

~17% of assessed spans fall outside the stated operating envelope and are auto-processed regardless. Either the envelope or the deployment is wrong; both cannot stand.

Performance claim predates two model changes

Vendor shipped two updates in 18 months under MSA §7.3. The recall figure was measured before both. No revalidation evidence exists for the currently deployed weights.

Shallow — cleared without human time

Oversight procedure predates the system

VM-114 was written in 2019 for manual patrol workflows and has not been revised for AI-generated work orders.

No named accountable owner

The file lists a vendor contact and a department. No individual is accountable for the system's continued fitness.

The review guardrail has no rejection criteria

“Planner reviews the queue” defines no basis on which a planner would reject a generated work order, and no record is kept when one is rejected.

42 Stage 06 · two peer utilities · week 12 · 60 min each

Three utilities, three defensible tiers, same evidence

Each reviewer is asked the single question the whole program turns on: would you deploy this in your territory on this evidence?

TIER 3

Nominating member

“A qualified planner reviews every work order before any crew is dispatched.”

Holds that a human decision gate sits between model output and physical action, which is what the autonomy dimension is meant to capture.

TIER 4

Reviewing member B

“At 180,000 spans a year that is twelve seconds per span. That is not review, it is a queue.”

Holds that oversight which cannot be exercised at the required throughput is not a control. Effective autonomy exceeds nominal autonomy.

TIER 3

Reviewing member C

“Tier 3, but only with stratified monitoring as a condition of the tier.”

Accepts the lower tier only if the unobservable-failure gap is closed — making the tier contingent on a control rather than on the architecture.

The disagreement is the deliverable. Under the old model this would be smoothed into consensus language in a working session. Here it is the most valuable output of the file, because it exposes a real hole: the rubric never distinguished documented oversight from achievable oversight.

2 · The vendor evidence request

Five specific asks — the same five any of the 25 members would now send, which is the point.

  • Recall stratified by species, terrain slope band, canopy density and acquisition season
  • Independent validation on a held-out sample from this territory
  • Change log for all model updates since deployment, with revalidation evidence per update

  • A supported false-negative sampling protocol
Twenty-five utilities asking for the same evidence package is where collective buying power converts into real market change — and it costs the group nothing beyond agreeing on the template.

46 Close

What to take from this

The gaps are the product, not the filled fields.

Seven sourced fields told the member nothing they did not already know. The four gaps produced an operating restriction, a vendor evidence request, a conditional registry entry, and two framework changes that now apply to all 25 members.

This is the credibility asset. “We ran this framework on ourselves first, and here is what it got wrong” is worth more to a skeptical utility CISO than any amount of framework prose.

The pattern, generalized

  • Agents draft, members decide. The agent's most valuable output was a refusal to fill four fields.
  • Order matters. Humans tier before seeing the copilot, or the calibration data measures model agreement instead of sector consensus.
  • Preserve dissent. The three-way tier split was not noise to be resolved — it was the sector's open question, made explicit.
  • Write the envelope. The single field no utility has today produced a three-year-old finding in seventy-five minutes.
  • Ask how failure becomes visible. If the answer is “it doesn't,” no other metric in the file can be trusted.
