Part IWhy SAFERai.power is needed
01

The situation: already deployed, and largely unassessed

The premise behind most AI governance programs is that AI is arriving. In utilities it has already arrived, in the least governed way possible: procured as a product feature, installed under an existing services contract, and operated by a department that does not think of itself as running an AI system.

Vegetation management, asset image analytics, AMI-based outage detection, load forecasting, call-center deflection, document review, field-work scheduling — these are not pilots. They are production systems with vendor contracts and operating procedures, and in many cases they predate the utility's first conversation about AI governance by several years.

Three properties of that installed base make it hard to govern with existing tools:

  • It was bought, not built. The evidence a utility would need sits with the vendor, and the contract rarely obliges them to produce it. The utility holds a model card and a security questionnaire; it does not hold stratified performance data or a change log.
  • Nobody wrote down the conditions of valid operation. The system was validated by the vendor on the vendor's data. Which conditions the claimed performance actually covers is usually not stated anywhere, by anyone.
  • Failures are often invisible by construction. When an AI system's error mode is an omission — a span not flagged, an anomaly not raised, a customer not contacted — there is typically no record that anything happened. A clean incident log is then evidence of nothing.
The observation that generalizes

The worked example's vegetation model reported 94% recall and had a three-year clean incident history. Both figures were true and neither was informative, because nothing in the pipeline could observe a miss. The first question a risk file should ask is not how well the system performs, but how its errors become visible.

This is what distinguishes utility AI assurance from the general problem. The task is not primarily to decide whether to adopt AI. It is to characterize what is already running, in an industry where the consequence of an unobserved failure can be a reliability event or an ignition.

02

The line that must be drawn first

There is a large, fast-moving 2026 regulatory workstream about AI and the grid that is not this program, and conflating the two is the most reliable way to lose executive attention. Leadership hears “AI and the grid,” maps it to the data-center interconnection fight, concludes it is already covered, and disengages.

Both workstreams matter. They have different owners, different deliverables and different clocks, and a member who sends the same person to both will find that the reliability-standards work crowds out the assurance work every time, because it has a filing deadline and this does not.

03

Five gaps no existing framework closes

The case for a new program is not that nothing exists. A great deal exists, and Part II is a primer on it. The case is that five specific things are missing, each one load-bearing, and no current body is producing them for this sector.

GAP 01
Nobody says what evidence is enough

NIST AI RMF tells you to measure. ISO/IEC 23894 tells you to run a risk process. Neither tells you what “good” looks like for a distribution-grid vision model that has to make a cut/no-cut call. There are no thresholds, no pass/fail, no sector context — by design, because these are frameworks rather than acceptance criteria.

Sector-specific acceptance criteria are the missing artifact, and they can only come from operators who know what they would and would not deploy.

Nobody currently produces this

GAP 02
The dominant failure mode has no home

The AI failures utilities will actually see are not malfunctions. They are cases where the system works exactly as designed and its performance is simply insufficient for the situation it met — the automotive world's SOTIF concept, ISO 21448.

NIST AI RMF has no vocabulary for this, and it is the failure mode that produces out-of-envelope operation, which is where most utility AI incidents will originate.

Exists in automotive · not translated to grid

GAP 03
Functional safety explicitly excludes AI

IEC 61508 and 61511 are the standards utilities already live inside for anything safety-related. AI has historically been disallowed as a technique for achieving functional safety, and SIL certification does not accommodate machine learning.

So the frameworks with real teeth in this industry say, in effect, not here — leaving everything outside a safety instrumented function ungoverned by anything comparable.

Deliberate exclusion · ISO/IEC TR 5469 is the bridge under construction

GAP 04
The one regulator that tried, declined

US banking has run examined model risk management since 2011. On 17 April 2026 the Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC jointly issued SR 26-2, superseding SR 11-7 — and it states that generative and agentic AI models “are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within the scope of this guidance.”

The most mature model-governance regime in any US sector looked at exactly the systems arriving in utility operations and left them to institutions.

Vacated ground · available to occupy

GAP 05
Nothing survives the model changing

Approval regimes assume a fixed artifact. Models get retrained, vendors ship updates on contractual notice periods, and foundation models change underneath the product. A typical master services agreement permits model updates on 30 days' notice with no revalidation requirement at all.

Medicine solved this with pre-authorized change envelopes. No utility framework has the concept, and without it any assurance program collapses under its own review burden.

Solved in medical devices · absent here

Each gap is closed somewhere else. None is closed here, and none will be closed by a body that does not operate a grid.

That is the actual argument for the program. It is not that utilities need an AI framework — they need the five or six specific judgments that turn a pile of adjacent-sector frameworks into something a planner can apply on a Tuesday, and those judgments are unavailable anywhere except from operators.

04

Why a consortium and not 25 programs

Every founding member could build this alone. Several are large enough to do it well. Three arguments say they should not.

1. The evidence problem is a buyer-side coordination problem

A single utility asking a vendor for stratified recall, an independent held-out validation and a documented change log is a customer with an unusual request. Twenty-five utilities asking for the same package, in the same format, is a market requirement. The cost of agreeing on the template is close to zero; the leverage it creates is not available to anyone acting alone.

This is the single highest-return item in the program, and it is pure coordination — no research, no tooling, no funding.

2. Calibration needs more than one operator

A rubric written and applied by one utility measures that utility's risk appetite. It becomes a standard only when independent operators tier the same system and you can see where they agree and where they do not. The disagreements are the output — they are the sector's genuine open questions, and they cannot be produced by a single organization at any budget.

3. The alternative to voluntary is not nothing

The nuclear industry built INPO after Three Mile Island because the alternative was worse. That is the honest framing for executive sponsorship here: a sector that can show a credible, operator-built assurance practice is in a different position, when the first significant AI-linked event occurs, than one that cannot.

The binding constraint on all of it

Roughly 80 hours of SME time per organization — about 2,000 hours across the founding cohort, or a shade under one person-year. That budget cannot author a standard. It can harvest, reconcile, test and ratify material that already exists, which is why Part II matters as much as Part I: the primer is the work plan.

The period over which those 80 hours are expected has not been established from any reachable source, and it is open question 1 for EPRI. It matters: the same 80 hours read as an MVP-cycle commitment or a multi-year one differ by a factor of four or more in weekly rate.

Part IIThe standards primer
06

How to read this: the layer model

The standards landscape looks like a pile because it is usually presented as one. It is more tractable as a stack: each layer answers a different question, and almost none of them compete with each other. A utility does not choose between ISO 42001 and UL 4600 any more than it chooses between a filing cabinet and an argument.

L1Management shellHow is the organization set up to govern AI at all? — NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, 23894, 42005, 38507, 5338
L2The argumentHow do you make a defensible claim about a system you cannot fully specify? — UL 4600, ISO 21448 (SOTIF), ODD
L3The change problemWhat happens when the model changes after approval? — FDA PCCP, GMLP
L4Inventory & proportionalityWhat do we have, and how much scrutiny does each one get? — SR 11-7 → SR 26-2
L5ContainmentWhere may it live and what may it touch? — IEC 62443, LOPA; IEC 61508/61511 as the exclusion boundary
L6AutonomyHow much authority does it have, and what must be true to give it more? — EASA AI levels, ARP6983/ED-324
L7Threats & guardrailsWhat will people do to it, and what stops them? — MITRE ATLAS, OWASP LLM Top 10, OWASP ASI
L8LearningHow does the sector find out what went wrong elsewhere? — ASIAS, INPO/EPIX
L9Regulatory perimeterWho can compel what? — EU AI Act, FERC/NERC, NERC CIP
How to use the layer model in practice

When someone asks “are we compliant with X?”, the useful reply is which layer X occupies and what the adjacent layers still need. ISO 42001 certification says a great deal about layer 1 and almost nothing about layers 2, 5 or 6 — which is exactly the confusion the stack is meant to prevent.

Each entry below follows the same shape: what it is, the idea worth taking, what it does not give you, and where SAFERai uses it.

07

Layer 1 — the management shell

This layer answers an organizational question, not a technical one: is there a named owner, a policy, a risk process, and a record? It is the layer executives and auditors recognize, and the one most likely to be mistaken for the whole job.

NIST · Framework · Voluntary

AI RMF 1.0 — GOVERN / MAP / MEASURE / MANAGE

Four core functions, broken into categories and roughly 72 subcategories. GOVERN alone has six categories and 19 subcategories; MAP has five and 18. GOVERN is organization-wide; MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE apply per system.

Take
The organizing spine and the vocabulary. The org-wide / per-system split is exactly the utility-plus-use-case structure this program needs.
Gap
Deliberately non-prescriptive. No thresholds, no pass/fail, no sector context. It is a completeness check, not a decision procedure.
Used as
The outer vocabulary, and the structure the crosswalk maps to.

NIST · Profile

NIST AI 600-1 — Generative AI Profile

Twelve GenAI-specific risk categories with suggested actions mapped back to the four RMF functions, organized around governance, content provenance, pre-deployment testing and incident disclosure.

Take
A ready-made risk taxonomy for the generative side, already aligned to RMF structure.
Gap
GenAI-general and not operational-technology aware. It says nothing about a system that can trip a breaker.
Used as
Input to the risk-scenario catalog, needing OT translation.

ISO/IEC · Certifiable

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI management system

The AI equivalent of ISO 27001: management-system clauses 4–10 covering context, leadership, planning, support, operation, evaluation and improvement, plus Annex A with 38 AI controls across 9 control objectives. Certifiable by third-party audit.

Take
The organizational shell, and a recognizable compliance object an executive or board already knows how to read.
Gap
Certifies that you have a system for managing AI. Says nothing about whether any particular AI system is fit to deploy.
Used as
The audit-facing shell; a reuse target so evidence is assembled once.

ISO/IEC · Guidance

ISO/IEC 23894 — AI risk management

Extends ISO 31000 risk-management practice to AI. This is the process detail that 42001 requires but does not itself specify.

Take
A defensible risk process that connects to enterprise risk language utilities already use.
Gap
Guidance only, not certifiable, and generic across sectors.
Used as
The process backbone under the risk file.

ISO/IEC · Guidance

ISO/IEC 42005:2025 — AI system impact assessment

Per-system impact assessment methodology: how to document what a specific AI system does, to whom, with what effects.

Take
The closest existing analogue to the AI System Risk File. Start here rather than from blank paper — this is the single highest-value harvest in layer 1.
Gap
Impact-oriented rather than fitness-oriented; no notion of an operating envelope or acceptance threshold.
Used as
The base template for the risk file's descriptive fields.

ISO/IEC · Guidance

ISO/IEC 38507 and 5338

38507 covers the governance implications of AI for the board. 5338 defines AI system lifecycle processes and stages.

Take
38507 for the executive framing; 5338 for stage definitions that give reassessment triggers something concrete to attach to.
Gap
Both are structural. Neither sets a threshold.
Used as
Board briefing language; the lifecycle vocabulary for triggers.
08

Layer 2 — the safety argument

This is the most important layer in the stack and the least familiar to utilities. It answers the question that makes AI assurance genuinely hard: how do you write a standard for a system whose behavior you cannot fully specify in advance?

Automotive · ANSI/UL · Published Apr 2020, now Edition 3

UL 4600 — Evaluation of Autonomous Products

Goal-based and technology-agnostic. Instead of specifying the system, it specifies the argument: a safety case built from goals (what it means to be safe in this context), argumentation (why the goals are met), and evidence. It is explicitly permissive about which underlying standards you conform to — ISO 26262, ISO 21448, IEC 61508, MIL-STD-882 — taking their outputs as inputs.

Take
The only mature model for a standard that works when requirements cannot be enumerated. It composes with, rather than competes against, everything else in this primer.
Gap
Written for products that move. The translation to a system that writes work orders or advises a dispatcher is the sector's job, not UL's.
Used as
The shape of the AI System Risk File — claim, argument, evidence — with the framework specifying required claims and acceptable evidence types by tier.

Automotive · ISO 21448

SOTIF — Safety Of The Intended Functionality

Covers hazards that arise when a system contains no fault and performs exactly as designed, but its performance is insufficient for the situation it encountered. The canonical example is a perception system that simply does not recognize an object class it was never trained on.

Take
The correct mental model for utility AI failure. Most incidents will not be malfunctions; they will be competent performance outside the conditions the evidence covered.
Gap
Has no natural home in NIST AI RMF language, which is why it goes missing from AI governance programs built on RMF alone.
Used as
The failure model behind the Operating Envelope and the observability-of-failure field.

AV & aviation practice · Concept

ODD → “Operating Envelope”

The Operational Design Domain is the explicitly bounded set of conditions under which the system is claimed to work — and, just as importantly, what happens outside them. It is a declaration, not a measurement.

Take
Rename it Operating Envelope and make it a mandatory risk-file field. It is the field most likely to be missing today, and the one most likely to produce a finding the first time anyone fills it in.
Gap
No utility practice currently names the envelope, so there is no existing document to harvest it from — this one has to be written.
Used as
A required field, and the boundary that determines whether an automated action is permitted at all.

Automotive · ISO/PAS 8800 · Emerging

ISO/PAS 8800 — road vehicles, safety and AI

A newer, narrower publicly available specification addressing safety for AI within road vehicles specifically.

Take
Worth tracking as the automotive sector's own attempt to bridge AI and functional safety — the same bridge ISO/IEC TR 5469 is building generically.
Gap
Vehicle-scoped and immature. Not harvestable yet.
Used as
Horizon-scan item.
Why this layer earns its place at the centre

Every other layer produces inputs. UL 4600 is the only thing in the primer that tells you what to do with them — how to assemble evidence into a claim someone can accept or reject. A risk file without an argument structure is a form; with one, it is a decision.

09

Layer 3 — the change problem

The hardest unsolved problem in regulated AI is not initial approval. It is that the thing you approved does not stay still.

Medical devices · FDA · Final guidance 3 Dec 2024

PCCP — Predetermined Change Control Plan

Authorized by FDORA 2022 and codified at FD&C Act §515C. A manufacturer pre-specifies, in the original submission, an envelope of future model changes: a description of modifications, a modification protocol, and an impact assessment. If FDA authorizes the plan, changes inside it can be implemented without a new marketing submission.

Take
The only mature, regulator-blessed answer to models that change. Adopt the pattern directly: what may change, by what method, validated how, with which monitoring.
Gap
Assumes a regulator to authorize the plan. In a voluntary consortium the authorizing party has to be defined — pod review, internal governance, or contract.
Used as
The change envelope field, and — reflexively — the change-control mechanism for the framework itself.

FDA · Health Canada · MHRA · 2021 · Principles

GMLP — Good Machine Learning Practice

Ten guiding principles covering multidisciplinary expertise, rigorous software engineering, representative datasets, independence of training and test sets, reference-standard quality, model design tailored to data and use, human-AI team performance, clinically relevant testing, clear user information, and post-deployment monitoring.

Take
Short, readable, sector-neutral and immediately adaptable. The human-AI team principle in particular anticipates the effective-versus-nominal oversight problem.
Gap
Principles, not requirements. No thresholds, no evidence format.
Used as
The capabilities-and-limitations and monitoring fields.
10

Layer 4 — inventory and proportionality

Banking has run mandatory, examined model risk management longer than anyone. Its lessons are process lessons, and they transfer almost without translation.

Banking · Fed/OCC · 2011–2026

SR 11-7 — the original model risk regime

Established the pattern every mature program still uses: a complete model inventory, risk tiering, independent validation proportional to risk, three lines of defense, and ongoing monitoring.

Take
The model inventory is the single most transferable artifact. Banks proved that a mandatory, complete, tiered inventory is the foundation everything else rests on.
Gap
Pre-AI, and written for quantitative finance models with far more tractable validation than a perception model.
Used as
The field structure for the AI System Registry.

Banking · Fed · OCC · FDIC · 17 Apr 2026

SR 26-2 — the revision, and what it declined

Supersedes SR 11-7 jointly across the three agencies. Three changes matter here: validation quality now turns on the rigor and effectiveness of the review rather than organizational structure; data is elevated to first-class risk, so validation must reach training data, lineage and representativeness; and the model definition tightens to complex systems, explicitly excluding spreadsheets, deterministic rules and ordinary software.

Take
All three. The validation loosening is a gift to utilities, which have far thinner validation benches than banks. The scope tightening is the template for keeping the framework from metastasizing across every script in the enterprise.
Gap
Generative and agentic AI are explicitly out of scope — “novel and rapidly evolving” — leaving institutions to apply their own governance.
Used as
The scope boundary, the proportionality principle, and the argument for occupying the vacated ground.
The line to use with a board

US banking regulators looked at governing generative and agentic AI and declined. A utility consortium that produces sector-specific guidance for exactly those systems is, on this narrow point, ahead of banking — which is a useful thing to be able to say, and true.

Layer 5 — containment

This layer is the one utilities are already best equipped for, and it is where the framework should put most of its weight as tier rises. Containment does not require predicting behavior. It constrains consequences regardless of behavior.

OT security · IEC 62443 series

IEC 62443 — zones and conduits

The industrial automation and control system security series. Its central structural idea is that a system is partitioned into zones — groupings with a common security level — communicating only through defined conduits with enforceable constraints. Security levels run 0–4 and are assigned per zone.

Take
The most directly reusable structural idea in the entire standards set. An AI agent is a thing that lives in a zone and communicates through a conduit. No new vocabulary is required, and every utility already has the architecture.
Gap
Written against a threat model of adversaries, not against a component that behaves unexpectedly while entirely un-compromised.
Used as
The deployment-conditions and guardrails field; the backbone of the guardrail catalog.

Process safety · CCPS · Method

LOPA — Layers of Protection Analysis

A semi-quantitative method in which each independent protection layer is credited with a specific risk reduction, and the layers must together close the gap between unmitigated and tolerable risk.

Take
The right mental model for a guardrail catalog: independent layers, each credited, rather than an undifferentiated list of controls.
Gap
AI layers are much harder to credit quantitatively than a relief valve. Expect ordinal credit, not probabilities.
Used as
The structure of the guardrail catalog — see clause 16.

Functional safety · IEC 61508 / 61511 · Exclusion

The boundary, not the toolkit

The functional-safety standards utilities already operate under. AI has historically been disallowed as a technique for achieving functional safety, and SIL certification does not accommodate machine learning.

Take
The exclusion itself. Assume any AI touching a safety instrumented function is out of scope for v1.0 and say so explicitly. A clear exclusion boundary is itself a valuable deliverable and prevents the framework being misread as authorization.
Gap
Leaves a large grey zone: systems that influence safety outcomes without being safety instrumented functions. That grey zone is most of the interesting cases.
Used as
The scope boundary's hardest edge.

ISO/IEC · TR 5469 · Emerging

ISO/IEC TR 5469 — functional safety and AI

A technical report addressing the use of AI in safety-related functions and the properties that would have to hold for it to be acceptable.

Take
The bridge under construction between AI and the functional-safety world. When it matures it changes the exclusion boundary above.
Gap
A technical report, not a standard. Not yet actionable.
Used as
Standing horizon-scan item with a named owner.

Layer 6 — the autonomy ladder

Aviation has spent years on the question this program will face in 2027: what must be true before a system is allowed to act without a human in the path?

Aviation · EASA · Issue 2 (2024) · Proposed Issue 3 (Jun 2026)

EASA AI levels — a ready-made ladder

Level 1 is human augmentation or assistance. Level 2 introduces human-AI teaming, split into 2A (directed decision-making, full authority retained by the end user) and 2B (supervised decision-making, with partial release of authority under full oversight). Issue 2 covers Levels 1 and 2. The proposed Issue 3 extends to Level 3, where the system has full authority to make and implement decisions — 3A under remote monitoring by an end user, 3B without end-user involvement.

Take
Adapt rather than invent. The 2A/2B and 3A/3B distinctions are exactly the granularity the autonomy dimension needs, and they were derived by a regulator that had to defend them.
Gap
Aircraft-centric. “Remote monitoring by an end user” means something different on a flight deck than in a distribution control room, and the translation is the sector's work.
Used as
The autonomy dimension of the tiering rubric, and the skeleton of the Release 2 autonomy ladder.

Aviation · EUROCAE WG-114 / SAE G-34

ARP6983 / ED-324 — certifying ML

A process standard for the development and certification approval of aeronautical products implementing AI, developed by a joint group of several hundred participants. The first version is scoped to frozen, supervised-learning models.

Take
The only serious attempt at a process standard for certifying ML, and a realistic model of what TEVV rigor looks like at the top tier.
Gap
That scope limit is the tell. After years of effort with substantial resources, aviation could certify only frozen models. Set expectations for what is achievable accordingly.
Used as
Reference for tier-4 TEVV depth; a calibration point for ambition.
The distinction the worked example forced into the rubric

An autonomy ladder describes documented authority. The vegetation case showed that documented and achievable authority can diverge sharply: an oversight control requiring review of 180,000 items a year by one planner is nominally human-on-the-loop and effectively autonomous. Any adaptation of the EASA levels needs a throughput test attached, or the ladder measures paperwork.

Layer 7 — threats and guardrails

These two catalogs are complementary and, between them, constitute most of a ready-made guardrail library. OWASP prioritizes what goes wrong; ATLAS supplies the techniques to test for and detect it.

MITRE · Catalog · Monthly releases

MITRE ATLAS

An ATT&CK-style adversary matrix for AI systems, covering the full lifecycle: training pipelines, model files, inference APIs and data supply chains. As of release 2026.07 it documents 16 tactics, 101 top-level techniques, 77 sub-techniques, 37 mitigations and 68 case studies, with agentic-AI techniques added through 2026.

Take
The technique catalog and the mitigations, directly. Also the case studies, which are the cheapest available source of real incident structure.
Gap
Adversary-focused. It does not address the un-attacked system that is simply wrong — which is layer 2's job.
Used as
The test catalog for TEVV, and half the guardrail catalog.

OWASP · Catalog

OWASP LLM Top 10 & Agentic Security Initiative

Application-layer risk prioritization for LLM systems, plus a dedicated agentic threat taxonomy spanning agent design, memory, planning and autonomy, tool use, and deployment/operations. The entries that matter most for utility agentic deployments are prompt injection, excessive agency, insecure plugin/tool design and excessive autonomy.

Take
The prioritization, and the agentic taxonomy — which is the closest thing available to a checklist for the systems arriving next.
Gap
Application-layer and IT-centric. Says nothing about consequence in a physical system.
Used as
Risk-scenario source; the agentic-systems annex.
A maintenance consequence, not just a content one

ATLAS now ships monthly releases versioned YYYY.MM. A guardrail catalog maintained by hand against a monthly upstream will be perpetually behind, and a crosswalk published as a PDF is untrustworthy within a year. This layer is the strongest argument for making the catalog agent-maintained on a schedule with human ratification, rather than document-released.

Layer 8 — learning from failure

This layer has the longest lead time, the highest long-term value, and the least technical content. It is legal work, and it should start in month one.

Aviation · FAA · Since 2007

ASIAS — Aviation Safety Information Analysis and Sharing

Fuses voluntarily reported safety data across government and industry. Its governing principle is the whole mechanism: information is used only for safety purposes, never for punitive action.

Take
The protection model. That guarantee is what makes the data flow; without it, the reporting channel exists and is empty.
Gap
Rests on a specific US aviation legal framework. The equivalent protections here have to be constructed, and that is not a technical task.
Used as
The design target for the non-punitive incident exchange.

Nuclear · INPO · Since 1979

INPO and EPIX

Confidential peer review, shared performance indicators, and an industry-wide component failure database — built voluntarily by the industry after Three Mile Island.

Take
Peer review as the durable mechanism, and the anonymized shared-indicator pattern for sector performance data.
Gap
Decades of institution-building behind it. Do not import the pace; do import the structure.
Used as
The model for cross-member red team, and for Release 4 sector indicators.
The reason this cannot wait

A reliability organization is a member of this consortium. Utilities will not discuss real AI incidents in a forum containing their regulator unless the protection is explicit, designed in from the start, and legally structured. Draft the protections before the first incident, not after — after is too late by definition, and the first incident sets the precedent for whether anyone ever reports a second.

Part IIIGovernance and guardrails
16

The guardrail taxonomy

A guardrail catalog organized as a flat list of controls is unusable. Organized as LOPA-style independent protection layers, it becomes something a risk file can cite and a reviewer can credit.

Two properties of this structure matter more than the specific entries:

  • It degrades gracefully as capability changes. A new model capability may invalidate a design-time constraint, but rate limits, confirmation gates and kill-switch ownership keep working. Pre-deployment assurance assumes you can characterize the system before it operates; guardrails do not.
  • It is cheap in the currency operations cares about. Bounded agents constrained by validation and optional human approval have been measured running far faster than manual operation. Guardrails cost very little speed — the point to make to an operations executive who expects assurance to mean delay.
17

The governance objects

Strip away the source standards and the framework is six objects and one rule. Members will interact with these, not with the primer.

ObjectWhat it isWhy it exists
Registry entryOne row per AI system, with tier, owner, envelope status, evidence completeness and next trigger. Banks proved a complete tiered inventory is the foundation everything rests on. The number-one registry failure mode is not bad schema but stale, incomplete data — so discovery has to be continuous, not an annual survey.
TierAn ordinal 1–4 rating from four dimensions: consequence, autonomy, operating context, exposure. Determines evidence depth, validation independence and monitoring intensity. Proportionality borrowed from SR 26-2; it is what keeps the framework affordable.
Risk fileA UL 4600-style safety case: claim, argument, evidence, across eleven required fields. The artifact a reviewer accepts or rejects. Everything else in the framework is either an input to it or a consequence of it.
Operating envelopeThe declared conditions of valid operation, and what happens outside them. Most incidents will be out-of-envelope operation. Undeclared, there is nothing to be outside of — and no basis for routing an out-of-envelope case to a human.
Change envelopeWhat may change without reassessment, what needs review, what needs full reassessment. Without it the framework is abandoned under review burden. It also converts a contractual notice period into an assurance control.
Named ratifierA specific human accountable for accepting each artifact. Agents draft; members decide. Nothing enters the framework on agent authority, and “a department” is not a ratifier.

Agents draft. Members decide. Nothing enters v1.0 on agent authority.

One field that is newer than the rest

Observability of failure — how do this system's errors become visible? — was not in the original field list. It came out of the first worked example, where a system with excellent reported metrics and a clean incident history turned out to have no mechanism capable of detecting a miss. “They don't” is an acceptable answer to the question, and an automatic escalation.

Read the right-hand column first

The middle column is harvestable — it is reading and extraction, and agents do most of it. The right-hand column is the entire value of the consortium, and none of it can be delegated. If a working session is not spending its time in the right-hand column, it is spending it wrongly.

Still assumed, and worth closing

  • The period over which the 80 SME hours are expected. No reachable source attaches a timeframe.   • EPRI-specific programme detail — governance model, phase boundaries, fee schedule. Direct retrieval of the SAFERai microsite was blocked by network policy during the original research, so this rests on search extraction rather than the pages themselves.
  • The EU AI Act Annex III date. The 7 May 2026 provisional agreement moving deployer obligations to 2 December 2027 was not formally published at the time of writing. Until it is, the earlier date stands on paper.
