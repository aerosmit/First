Layer 1

The management shell

How is the organization set up to govern AI at all? The layer executives recognize — and the one most often mistaken for the whole job.
NIST AI RMF 1.0NIST AI 100-1 · 2023Free

AI Risk Management Framework

Four core functions — GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE — broken into categories and roughly 72 subcategories. GOVERN is organization-wide; the other three apply per system, which is exactly the utility-plus-use-case split this sector needs.

Leaves: deliberately non-prescriptive. No thresholds, no pass/fail, no sector context. A vocabulary and a completeness check, not a decision procedure.

airc.nist.gov — AI RMF Core · Playbook
NIST AI 600-1NIST · Jul 2024Free

Generative AI Profile

Twelve generative-AI risk categories with suggested actions mapped back to the four RMF functions — organized around governance, content provenance, pre-deployment testing and incident disclosure.

Leaves: generative-AI-general and not operational-technology aware. Says nothing about a system that can trip a breaker.

nvlpubs.nist.gov — NIST.AI.600-1.pdf
NIST COSAISSP 800-53 overlays · concept paper Aug 2025In progress

Control Overlays for Securing AI Systems

A planned series of SP 800-53 control overlays for AI. Would connect AI risk to the control language utility security teams already speak — which is the cheapest possible on-ramp for a CISO organization.

Status: concept paper and action plan open for comment. An early sector contribution here is high-leverage, and the crosswalk work produces the material as a by-product.

csrc.nist.gov/projects/cosais · Concept paper (PDF)
ISO/IEC 42001:2023ISO/IEC · 2023PaidCertifiable

AI management system (AIMS)

The AI equivalent of ISO 27001. Management-system clauses 4–10 covering context, leadership, planning, support, operation, evaluation and improvement, plus Annex A with 38 AI controls across 9 control objectives. Certifiable by third-party audit.

Leaves: certifies that you have a system for managing AI. Says nothing about whether any particular AI system is fit to deploy.

iso.org — ISO/IEC 42001:2023
ISO/IEC 23894:2023ISO/IEC · 2023 · ~CHF 155Paid

Guidance on risk management for AI

Extends ISO 31000 risk-management practice to AI. This is the process detail that 42001 requires but does not itself specify, and it connects to enterprise risk language utilities already use.

Leaves: guidance only, not certifiable, generic across sectors.

iso.org catalogue — 23894
ISO/IEC 42005:2025ISO/IEC · 2025 · ~CHF 181Paid

AI system impact assessment

Per-system methodology for documenting what a specific AI system does, to whom, with what effects.

Take: the closest existing analogue to the AI System Risk File. Start here rather than from blank paper — the single highest-value harvest in this layer. Leaves: impact-oriented rather than fitness-oriented; no operating envelope, no acceptance threshold.

iso.org catalogue — 42005
ISO/IEC 38507:2022ISO/IEC · 2022 · ~CHF 179Paid

Governance implications of AI for the board

Board-level framing: what directors need to understand and decide about organizational use of AI.

Use for: the executive and board layer of a briefing. Structural — sets no thresholds.

iso.org catalogue — 38507
ISO/IEC 5338:2023ISO/IEC · 2023 · ~CHF 181Paid

AI system life cycle processes

Defines AI lifecycle stages and processes, extending the software lifecycle standards to AI.

Use for: stage definitions that give reassessment triggers something concrete to attach to.

iso.org catalogue — 5338
ISO/IEC 22989:2022ISO/IEC · 2022Paid

AI concepts and terminology

The vocabulary standard the rest of the ISO/IEC AI set is written against.

Use for: settling definitional arguments cheaply. Worth one copy for the group rather than one per member.

iso.org catalogue — 22989
Layer 2

The safety argument

How do you write a standard for a system whose behaviour you cannot fully specify in advance? The most important layer here, and the least familiar to utilities.
ANSI/UL 4600UL Solutions · 2020, now Ed. 3Paid

Evaluation of Autonomous Products

Goal-based and technology-agnostic. Instead of specifying the system it specifies the argument: a safety case built from goals, argumentation and evidence. Explicitly permissive about which underlying standards you conform to — ISO 26262, ISO 21448, IEC 61508, MIL-STD-882 — taking their outputs as inputs.

Take: the only mature model for a standard that works when requirements cannot be enumerated, and the shape the AI System Risk File should have. Leaves: written for products that move; the translation to a system that advises a dispatcher is the sector's job.

ul.com — Edition 3 · OECD.AI entry
ISO 21448:2022ISO · 2022Paid

SOTIF — Safety Of The Intended Functionality

Covers hazards arising when a system contains no fault and performs exactly as designed, but its performance is insufficient for the situation it encountered.

Take: the correct mental model for utility AI failure. Most incidents will not be malfunctions; they will be competent performance outside the conditions the evidence covered. Leaves: has no natural home in NIST AI RMF language, which is why programmes built on RMF alone miss it entirely.

iso.org catalogue — 21448
ISO/PAS 8800ISO · publicly available specPaid

Road vehicles — safety and artificial intelligence

The automotive sector's own attempt to bridge AI and functional safety — narrower and newer than UL 4600.

Status: vehicle-scoped and immature. A horizon-scan item rather than a harvest target, tracked alongside ISO/IEC TR 5469.

iso.org catalogue — 8800
The concept with no standard attached

Operational Design Domain (ODD) — the explicitly bounded conditions under which a system is claimed to work, and what happens outside them. It comes from AV and aviation practice rather than from a single citable standard, and it is carried inside UL 4600 and ISO 21448 work. Rename it “Operating Envelope” and make it a mandatory risk-file field — it is the field most likely to be missing today, and the one most likely to produce a finding the first time anyone fills it in.

Layer 3

The change problem

The hardest unsolved problem in regulated AI is not initial approval. It is that the thing you approved does not stay still.
FDA PCCP guidanceUS FDA · final 3 Dec 2024Free

Predetermined Change Control Plan for AI-enabled device software functions

Authorized by FDORA 2022, codified at FD&C Act §515C. A manufacturer pre-specifies, in the original submission, an envelope of future model changes: a description of modifications, a modification protocol, and an impact assessment. Once authorized, changes inside the plan need no new marketing submission.

Take: the only mature, regulator-blessed answer to models that change. Adopt the pattern directly as the risk file's change envelope. Leaves: assumes a regulator to authorize the plan — in a voluntary consortium the authorizing party has to be defined.

fda.gov — PCCP final guidance
GMLPFDA · Health Canada · MHRA · 27 Oct 2021Free

Good Machine Learning Practice — 10 guiding principles

Multidisciplinary expertise, rigorous software engineering, representative datasets, independence of training and test sets, reference-standard quality, model design tailored to data and use, human-AI team performance, clinically relevant testing, clear user information, and post-deployment monitoring.

Take: short, readable, sector-neutral, immediately adaptable. The human-AI team principle anticipates the effective-versus-nominal oversight problem by several years.

fda.gov — GMLP · Health Canada mirror · MHRA mirror
Layer 4

Inventory and proportionality

What do we have, and how much scrutiny does each one get? Banking has run this longer than anyone, and the lessons transfer almost without translation.
SR 26-2Fed · OCC · FDIC · 17 Apr 2026Free

Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management

Supersedes SR 11-7 and SR 21-8, issued jointly across the three agencies. Three changes matter here: validation quality now turns on the rigor and effectiveness of the review rather than organizational structure; data is elevated to first-class risk, so validation must reach training data, lineage and representativeness; and the model definition tightens to complex systems, explicitly excluding spreadsheets, deterministic rules and ordinary software.

The gift: it states that generative and agentic AI “are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within the scope of this guidance.” The most mature model-risk regime in any US sector looked at exactly these systems and declined.

federalreserve.gov — SR 26-2 · Attachment (PDF)
SR 11-7Fed · 2011 · superseded 2026Free

Guidance on Model Risk Management

The original regime, and the pattern every mature programme still uses: a complete model inventory, risk tiering, independent validation proportional to risk, three lines of defense, ongoing monitoring.

Take: the model inventory is the single most transferable artifact in this whole reference — copy its field structure for the AI System Registry. Still worth reading despite being superseded, because SR 26-2 assumes you know it.

federalreserve.gov — SR 11-7
Layer 5

Containment

Where may it live and what may it touch? The layer utilities are already best equipped for — and where the framework should put most of its weight as tier rises.
ISA/IEC 62443ISA / IEC · seriesPaid

Security for industrial automation and control systems

Its central structural idea: a system is partitioned into zones — groupings with a common security level — communicating only through defined conduits with enforceable constraints. Security levels run 0–4, assigned per zone.

Take: the most directly reusable structural idea in the entire standards set. An AI agent is a thing that lives in a zone and talks through a conduit. No new vocabulary required, and every utility already has the architecture. Leaves: written against a threat model of adversaries, not a component that behaves unexpectedly while entirely un-compromised.

isa.org — ISA/IEC 62443 series
IEC 61508IEC · functional safetyPaid

Functional safety of E/E/PE safety-related systems

The parent functional-safety standard, and the source of Safety Integrity Levels.

Take the exclusion, not the toolkit. AI has historically been disallowed as a technique for achieving functional safety, and SIL certification does not accommodate machine learning. Assume any AI touching a safety instrumented function is out of scope for v1.0 and say so explicitly — a clear exclusion boundary is itself a valuable deliverable.

webstore.iec.ch — search 61508
IEC 61511IEC · process sectorPaid

Safety instrumented systems for the process industry

The process-sector application of 61508 — the standard most utility safety engineers actually work to.

Leaves: a large grey zone — systems that influence safety outcomes without being safety instrumented functions. That grey zone is most of the interesting cases.

webstore.iec.ch — search 61511
ISO/IEC TR 5469ISO/IEC · technical reportPaid

Functional safety and AI systems

Addresses the use of AI in safety-related functions and what would have to hold for it to be acceptable.

Take: the bridge under construction between AI and the functional-safety world utilities already live in. When it matures it moves the exclusion boundary above. Status: a technical report, not a standard. Standing horizon-scan item with a named owner.

iso.org catalogue — 5469
LOPACCPS / AIChE · methodPaid

Layers of Protection Analysis

A semi-quantitative method in which each independent protection layer is credited with a specific risk reduction, and the layers together must close the gap between unmitigated and tolerable risk.

Take: the right structure for a guardrail catalog — independent layers, each credited — rather than an undifferentiated list of controls. Leaves: AI layers are much harder to credit quantitatively than a relief valve. Expect ordinal credit, not probabilities.

aiche.org/ccps — CCPS
Layer 6

The autonomy ladder

How much authority does it have, and what must be true to give it more? Aviation has spent years on the question this sector faces in 2027.
EASA AI Concept PaperEASA · Issue 2, 2024Free

Guidance for Level 1 & 2 machine-learning applications

Level 1 is human augmentation or assistance. Level 2 is human-AI teaming, split into 2A (directed decision-making, full authority retained by the end user) and 2B (supervised decision-making, partial release of authority under full oversight).

Take: adapt rather than invent. The 2A/2B distinction is exactly the granularity the autonomy dimension needs, and it was derived by a regulator that had to defend it. Leaves: aircraft-centric — the translation to a control room is the sector's work.

easa.europa.eu — Issue 2
EASA Issue 3 (proposed)EASA · Jun 2026Free

Guidance extended to Level 3 — full authority

Extends the ladder to Level 3, where the system has full authority to make and implement decisions: 3A under remote monitoring by an end user, 3B without end-user involvement.

Take: this is the newest and most directly useful item in the whole reference. The autonomy ladder scheduled for Release 2 now has a published aviation definition of full authority to adapt rather than invent. Status: proposed issue, out for comment.

easa.europa.eu — proposed Issue 03 (PDF)
ARP6983 / ED-324SAE G-34 · EUROCAE WG-114In progress

Process standard for development and certification of aeronautical products implementing AI

A joint SAE/EUROCAE process standard developed by several hundred participants, still in development. The first version is scoped to frozen, supervised-learning models.

Take: the only serious attempt at a process standard for certifying ML, and a realistic model of tier-4 TEVV rigor. That scope limit is the tell — after years of effort with substantial resources, aviation scoped its first version to frozen models. Calibrate ambition accordingly, and track the release date rather than assuming it is available.

sae.org — ARP6983
Layer 7

Threats and guardrails

What will people do to it, and what stops them? Between them, roughly 80% of a ready-made guardrail catalog — and both are free.
MITRE ATLASMITRE · release 2026.07 · monthlyFree

Adversarial Threat Landscape for AI Systems

An ATT&CK-style adversary matrix covering the full AI lifecycle — training pipelines, model files, inference APIs, data supply chains. As of release 2026.07 it documents 16 tactics, 101 top-level techniques, 77 sub-techniques, 37 mitigations and 68 case studies, with agentic-AI techniques added through 2026.

Take: the technique catalog, the mitigations, and the case studies — the cheapest available source of real incident structure. Note the cadence: ATLAS now ships monthly, versioned YYYY.MM. A hand-maintained crosswalk cannot track it; this is the strongest argument for an agent-maintained catalog.

atlas.mitre.org · github.com/mitre-atlas/atlas-data
OWASP Top 10 for LLM AppsOWASP GenAI Security Project · 2025Free

Application-layer risk prioritization for LLM systems

The 2025 edition: LLM01 prompt injection, LLM02 sensitive information disclosure, LLM03 supply chain, LLM04 data and model poisoning, LLM05 improper output handling, LLM06 excessive agency, LLM07 system prompt leakage, LLM08 vector and embedding weaknesses, LLM09 misinformation, LLM10 unbounded consumption.

For utility deployments the load-bearing entries are LLM01, LLM06 and LLM08 — the last one being exactly the retrieval failure the ASK-1 worked example turns on. Leaves: application-layer and IT-centric; says nothing about consequence in a physical system.

genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10 · 2025 edition
OWASP Agentic SecurityOWASP GenAI Security ProjectFree

Agentic Security Initiative — threats and mitigations

A dedicated agentic threat taxonomy spanning agent design, memory, planning and autonomy, tool use, and deployment/operations.

Take: the closest thing available to a checklist for the systems arriving next — and the natural source for the agentic annex, given that banking's regulators declined to cover them.

genai.owasp.org — Agentic Security Initiative
Layer 8

Learning from failure

Longest lead time, highest long-term value, least technical content. This layer is legal work, and it should start in month one.
FAA ASIASFAA · since 2007Free

Aviation Safety Information Analysis and Sharing

Fuses voluntarily reported safety data across government and industry. Its governing principle is the whole mechanism: information is used only for safety purposes, never for punitive action.

Take: the protection model. That guarantee is what makes data flow; without it the reporting channel exists and is empty. Leaves: rests on a specific US aviation legal framework — the equivalent here has to be constructed, and that is not a technical task.

faa.gov — ASIAS · asias.faa.gov
INPO / EPIXINPO · since 1979 · members onlyMembers

Peer review, shared indicators, equipment failure exchange

Confidential peer review, shared performance indicators, and an industry-wide component failure database — built voluntarily by the nuclear industry after Three Mile Island.

Take: peer review as the durable mechanism, and the anonymized shared-indicator pattern. Also the right narrative for executive sponsorship — the industry built it voluntarily because the alternative was worse. Leaves: decades of institution-building behind it. Import the structure, not the pace.

inpo.info
Layer 9

The regulatory perimeter

What can actually be compelled, by whom, and when.
EU AI ActRegulation (EU) 2024/1689Free

Annex III §2 — critical infrastructure as high-risk

Classifies as high-risk any AI system intended as a safety component in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or the supply of water, gas, heating and electricity. Deployer obligations sit in Articles 9–17 and 26; penalties reach €15M or 3% of global turnover. Article 6(3) provides a documented-assessment exception for systems that do not pose significant risk.

Watch the date. Annex III deployer obligations were set for 2 August 2026; a provisional political agreement of 7 May 2026 moves this to 2 December 2027. Until formal publication the earlier date stands on paper. Affects any member with EU operations and every EU-domiciled vendor in the room.

eur-lex.europa.eu — full text · EC AI Act Service Desk — Annex III
NERC CIPNERC · mandatory in North AmericaFree

Critical Infrastructure Protection standards

Not AI-specific, but its concepts — BES Cyber System categorization, access control, change management, incident reporting — map onto AI system classification with moderate translation effort.

Take: NERC's presence in the SAFERai membership makes that mapping credible rather than speculative. Also worth knowing: ISA reports that a large majority of CIP technical controls can be evidenced through IEC 62443 assessments, which makes the layer-5 harvest do double duty.

nerc.com — Reliability Standards
NERC Project 2026-02FERC order 16 Jul 2026Free

Reliability standards for computational loads

FERC directed NERC to file mandatory standards governing integration of large computational loads — GenAI data centres, crypto, IT facilities — by 31 December 2026, with a Phase II work plan due 1 March 2027.

This is AI as a load, not AI as a tool. Adjacent, not applicable. Track it so you can draw the distinction confidently in the first two minutes of any briefing — conflating the two is the most common way this programme loses executive attention.

nerc.com — Project 2026-02
NERC Level 3 AlertNERC · 4 May 2026Free

AI campuses and hyperscale data centres as dynamic grid actors

Seven Essential Actions covering load modeling, system studies, commissioning, ride-through analysis, fault recording and operational communications.

Same caveat: load-side, not tool-side. Included here so the boundary is documented rather than assumed.

Where to start

Six items, all free except one, that give you eighty percent of the argument in a weekend.
1 · UL 4600The only one worth paying for first. Read it for the safety-case structure, not the automotive content.
2 · SR 26-2Twenty minutes. The out-of-scope language for generative and agentic AI is the single most useful paragraph in this reference.
3 · FDA PCCPThe change envelope, fully worked, by a regulator who had to make it stick.
4 · GMLP 10 principlesTwo pages. The fastest way to sound fluent in model assurance.
5 · OWASP LLM Top 10Free, current, and the vocabulary everyone deploying generative AI is already using.
6 · EASA Issue 3The newest thing here. Level 3A/3B is the autonomy language the sector will need in 2027.
What this set costs

Everything in layers 3, 4, 7, 8 and 9 is free — that is the FDA, Federal Reserve, OWASP, MITRE, FAA, EU and NERC material, and it is most of the argument. The paid items are concentrated in layers 1, 2 and 5 — ISO/IEC, UL and IEC — and typically run CHF 150–200 each. A single shared set for the consortium is far cheaper than one per member, and worth raising as a group purchase in week one.

Version notes and corrections

Four things that have moved, and which older summaries get wrong.
Check these against any earlier list you hold
  • OWASP numbering changed in 2025. Lists citing “LLM07 insecure plugin design” or “LLM08 excessive autonomy” are on the 2023 edition. In the current list LLM07 is system prompt leakage and LLM08 is vector and embedding weaknesses; excessive agency is LLM06.
  • FDA PCCP final guidance is dated 3 December 2024, not 2025.
  • MITRE ATLAS now ships monthly as YYYY.MM and has grown well past the v5.1.0 figures still widely quoted — 101 techniques and 68 case studies as of 2026.07.
  • EASA has a proposed Issue 3 (June 2026) defining Level 3A and 3B. Summaries that stop at “Issue 2, Levels 1 and 2” are a version behind on the most important deliverable in the 2027 roadmap.
