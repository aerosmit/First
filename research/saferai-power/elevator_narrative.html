The seventeen percent

One story, cut to four lengths — under twenty seconds to two and a half minutes. The same system, the same numbers, the same turn. Learn the shortest one cold and the others become expansions of it rather than different speeches.

01 · The premise

Why this story and not a description of the program

Nobody remembers a framework. They remember that a number everyone trusted turned out to mean nothing, and that nobody had done anything wrong.

The story works because it has a reversal and no villain. A reversal makes it stick; the absence of a villain is what makes a utility executive lean in rather than get defensive — because they are hearing about their own systems, and they know it by the second sentence.

Three numbers carry the whole thing. Do not add a fourth. Every extra number you introduce costs you one of these.

94%The reassuring number. Reported accuracy, three years, clean record.
17%The turn. Share of the grid operating outside conditions nobody had ever written down.
3.5 hrsThe relief. What it costs one person to find that.
The one line that does the most work

“Nobody did anything wrong.” Without it the story is an exposé and your listener gets defensive on behalf of their own operation. With it, the story is about a systemic blind spot they share — and they start telling you about their version of it. That reaction is the entire goal.

02 · Under twenty seconds

For “so what do you work on?”

Problemalready there

Utilities are already running AI that nobody ever assessed as AI.

Answerwho + what

We're getting twenty-five of them to agree on how you prove one is safe enough to deploy — and what evidence to demand from the vendor.

This is the version you will use most. It ends on a concrete noun — evidence from a vendor — which is what makes people ask a follow-up instead of nodding politely.

The mechanics of why it lands

The first line is a picture, not a category — trees, power lines. The second lulls. The third is the whole speech: “the conditions it's actually valid in” is a phrase nobody in the industry can currently produce for their own systems, and the good listeners realise that while you're still talking.

05 · Two and a half minutes

The meeting opener

Use this to open a briefing, or when someone has genuinely made room. It adds the two things the shorter versions cut: what the utility actually did about it, and why this has to be a consortium rather than a project.

2:36 The full arc 339 words

Frame

When people say AI and the grid, they mean data centres. I work on the other one — the AI inside the utility. Vegetation management, outage detection, load forecasting, inspections. It's already running, and most of it was never assessed as AI at all, because it arrived as a feature of something the utility bought.

Case

Here's a system that makes the point. A utility flies twelve thousand circuit miles of distribution line every year with LiDAR. A vendor's vision model reads the imagery, decides which spans have trees too close to the conductor, and writes the work orders straight into the work management system. A planner reviews the queue, crews go cut. Three years in production. Ninety-four percent recall. No AI-attributed incidents.

Methodhow it runs

Running that through what we're building takes about three and a half hours of the utility's own people. Software pre-fills what it can from documents they already have — the vendor's model card, the contract, the operating procedure — and, more usefully, flags what's missing.

Turn 1the envelope

Two things came out. First: nobody had ever written down the conditions under which that model is claimed to work. When they did — leaf-on season, slope under twenty-five degrees, species it was trained on — seventeen percent of assessed spans fell outside it. Steep terrain, post-fire regrowth. Auto-processed anyway. For three years.

Turn 2unverifiable

Second: the ninety-four percent couldn't be checked. The system has a record of every tree it flagged. It has no mechanism at all for a tree it missed.

Absolveno villain

Nobody did anything wrong. The boundary had simply never been written down, so there was nothing to be outside of.

Resolvewhat changed

They didn't switch it off. They restricted automatic work orders to the spans inside the envelope, routed the rest to manual patrol, started sampling unflagged spans to actually measure misses, and sent the vendor five specific evidence requests.

Scalewhy twenty-five

And that's why it's twenty-five utilities and not one. Those five requests are now the same five every member sends. That moves the market.

06 · Delivery

How to say it

Do

Stop after “clean record”

A full beat. You are setting a trap and the pause is what makes the listener commit to the comfortable reading before you turn it over.

Do

Slow down on “nobody did anything wrong”

Half your normal pace. It is the line that converts a listener from defensive to curious, and it only works if it sounds deliberate rather than thrown away.

Do

Let the concrete details do the work

Trees. Steep ground. Burn scars. A crew with a saw. These are what make it real — far more than “computer vision model” ever will.

Do

End on a noun, not a mission

“Evidence from a vendor.” “Three and a half hours.” “The same five requests.” Concrete endings invite questions; aspirational ones invite nodding.

Don't

Use a single acronym

No SOTIF, no ODD, no UL 4600, no TEVV, no tiering rubric. Every one of them is a request that your listener stop following the story and start decoding. The whole point is that the story carries the concepts without naming them.

Don't

Open with what the program is

“SAFERai.power is an EPRI-led collaborative that will develop a risk framework…” loses the room in nine words. The program is the last thing you say, once they want to know who's fixing it.

Don't

Add a fourth number

Ninety-four, seventeen, three and a half. Adding 12,400 miles is fine as colour in the longer versions; adding 180,000 spans, 25 members and 2,000 hours all at once means they remember none of them.

Don't

Claim it's a real utility

It's a composite built from patterns common to vegetation programs. Say “here's the shape of it” or “a system like this.” If someone asks directly, answer directly — in a room full of people who run these systems, a fudge is expensive and the story is just as good as an archetype.

07 · Interrupts

When they cut in — which means it worked

Getting interrupted is success. Have these ready at about fifteen seconds each, then stop and let them steer.

They ask

“Isn't that just model validation?”

Validation tells you how the model scored on the vendor's test set. This asks a different question — under what conditions is that score claimed to hold, and how would you ever find out you were outside them. Most systems can answer the first and nothing answers the second.

They ask

“How's this different from the data centre stuff?”

Completely different problem. That's AI as a load on the grid — FERC and NERC are already on it. This is AI as a tool inside the utility, making operational decisions. Same two words, nothing else in common.

They ask

“What would you need from me?”

Two real systems you already have running, and about eighty hours of one good person's time. Not a committee seat — two actual systems, with their actual gaps. You get the assessment on your own systems either way.

They ask

“Who else is in it?”

About twenty-eight organisations. Most of the big investor-owned utilities, public power, the market operators — and NERC. Plus the technology vendors, which matters, because the point is agreeing what evidence they have to hand over.

They ask

“Doesn't this slow everything down?”

It's the opposite problem. Right now people either deploy with no basis or stall for months arguing. Three and a half hours to a documented decision is faster than the argument you're having today — and it's defensible afterwards.

They ask

“So what happened to that system?”

Still running. They restricted the automatic work orders to the ground the model was validated on, sent the rest to manual patrol, and started sampling the spans it didn't flag — so for the first time they can actually measure a miss.

08 · Variants

Same story, different last line

Never rewrite the narrative for the audience. Change only the closing sentence — the story is doing the persuading, and the last line just points it at what they own.

CEO / board

This is how you say yes to AI faster, and be able to explain the yes afterwards.

CISO / risk

You get a documented boundary and a monitoring trigger, instead of a vendor's aggregate number.

Operations

The crews still cut the trees. The difference is you know which spans nobody is looking at.

Vendor / technology member

Build the evidence package once, and it satisfies twenty-five buyers instead of twenty-five questionnaires.

Regulator / NERC

The sector is documenting its own failure modes before anybody has to ask it to.

Prospective member

Eighty hours of one person's time — and you take the whole method home and run it on your own portfolio.

If you only remember one thing

Open with the trees, pause after “clean record,” turn on “the conditions it's actually valid in,” and slow down for “nobody did anything wrong.” Everything else is expansion.
